Regular physical activity and high cardiorespiratory fitness are among the strongest modifiable predictors of low cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [Garcia 2023; Lang 2024; Lee 2022]. Exercise is therefore a cornerstone of public-health and rehabilitation strategies [Bjarnason-Wehrens 2023; Lucini 2021]. Its benefits depend on the dose, and intensity is the component of the dose that is hardest to prescribe and control. Intensity is usually prescribed and monitored in terms of external load, such as cycling power or running speed. However, the same external load can impose a very different internal load between individuals, and within the same individual from one day to the next, depending on fitness, efficiency, fatigue, environment and nutrition [Impellizzeri 2019]. Prescribing an intensity that is both safe and effective is particularly difficult in sedentary and clinical populations [Lehtonen 2022; Meyler 2021; Taylor 2023]. Heart rate (HR) is the most widely used marker of internal load, but its relationship with metabolic rate drifts during prolonged exercise and is altered by heat, hydration status and fatigue [Achten 2003]. A wearable measure that more directly reflects the metabolic cost of exercise would therefore be valuable for training, rehabilitation and research.

Pulmonary oxygen uptake (V̇O₂) is the reference measure of aerobic metabolic rate. During steady-state exercise it reflects the oxygen requirement of the working muscles and other tissues. After a change in work rate it rises or falls with a delay set by pulmonary gas exchange, convective O₂ transport and muscle O₂ utilisation, and above the gas exchange threshold its kinetics slow and a slow component develops [Poole 2012; Rossiter 2011]. Continuous V̇O₂ therefore carries information about both the magnitude and the dynamics of the metabolic response. Measuring it, however, requires a metabolic cart or portable gas analyser with a face mask or mouthpiece, regular calibration and controlled procedures [Glaab 2022; Poole 2012]. This confines direct measurement to laboratories and clinics. Practical proxies such as pedometers and accelerometers are convenient but estimate energy expenditure with considerable error, especially across activities and intensities [Choi 2019; Vähä-Ypyä 2023].

Wearable sensors can now record several physiological signals that are mechanistically linked to V̇O₂ with little intrusion. HR reflects the cardiovascular response and relates to V̇O₂ through cardiac output and the Fick principle [Achten 2003]. Chest straps and smart garments estimate minute ventilation (V̇E) and breathing rate (BR), which are closely coupled to pulmonary gas exchange [Albouaini 2007; Clarenbach 2005]. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) measures the oxygenation of the underlying muscle, which reflects the local balance between O₂ delivery and O₂ utilisation [Barstow 2019; Perrey 2024]. Unlike HR and V̇E, which reflect the central, whole-body response, NIRS reflects the peripheral site where oxygen is consumed. In cycling, external work rate is also available from instrumented ergometers or power meters, and static participant characteristics such as sex and skeletal muscle mass relate to the scale of the V̇O₂ response. None of these signals is a direct measure of V̇O₂, and their relationships with it are non-linear, history-dependent and individual. Machine-learning models are well suited to learn such mappings from data [Amelard 2021; Beltrame 2017; Zignoli 2020].

A growing body of work has shown that this approach is feasible. Random-forest models predicted V̇O₂ from wearable signals during activities of daily living, including unsupervised activities [Beltrame 2017; Beltrame 2018], and V̇O₂ during non-steady-state activities and transitions has been estimated from wearable sensors [Altini 2016]. In cycling, artificial and recurrent neural networks estimated V̇O₂ from easy-to-obtain inputs [Borror 2019; Zignoli 2020]. Amelard et al. showed that a temporal convolutional network (TCN), which models the recent history of its inputs through causal dilated convolutions [Bai 2018], predicted the dynamic V̇O₂ response across exercise intensities more accurately than random-forest and long short-term memory models, at lower computational cost [Amelard 2021]. Hedge et al. applied the same architecture to moderate- and heavy-intensity cycling in which work rate switched pseudo-randomly between two levels (pseudorandom binary sequence, PRBS), and reported a repeated-measures correlation of 0.974 between predicted and measured V̇O₂ [Hedge 2023]. Gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks have also been applied to cycling V̇O₂ [Jun 2024], and related temporal models are used to estimate energy expenditure from wearables [Paraschiakos 2022; Zhou 2022]. These results establish that sequential deep-learning models can track V̇O₂ closely from wearable data under laboratory conditions.

Two questions nevertheless remain open. The first is **which input signals are actually needed**. Studies differ in the sensors they use, and few have compared modalities systematically within one dataset. Amelard et al. and Hedge et al. relied on HR and on V̇E from a smart shirt calibrated against the metabolic cart for each participant [Amelard 2021; Hedge 2023]. That calibration step requires the very equipment the wearable is meant to replace. Whether uncalibrated ventilation from a commercial strap carries the same information is unknown. Peripheral muscle oxygenation from NIRS has rarely been tested as an input, although it reflects the site of O₂ consumption directly. In cycling, work rate is a strong predictor on its own, because the V̇O₂ gain for moderate exercise is relatively stable at roughly 9–11 mL·min⁻¹·W⁻¹ [Poole 2012; Rossiter 2011]. It is therefore unclear how much physiological signals add once work rate is known, and how well they perform without it, as would be necessary in activities where external power cannot be measured. Two preliminary studies in our laboratories addressed parts of this question and reached partly diverging conclusions. In 14 participants performing PRBS cycling at three intensity combinations, the most accurate model combined work rate, HR, heart-rate reserve, Hb-diff and anthropometrics, and accurate prediction remained possible without work rate **[cite Gies thesis/report if citable]**. In 13 participants performing four protocols with different temporal structures, work rate alone was the strongest single input, and no combination performed significantly better **[cite Jansen 2026 thesis]**. Because the two studies differed in data cleaning, feature definitions (e.g. heart-rate reserve and NIRS normalisation), scaling, training settings and model selection, their conclusions cannot be reconciled from the published numbers.

The second question is **how reliably modality comparisons can be made at all with the small datasets typical of this field**. Studies of V̇O₂ prediction typically include one cohort of roughly 10–20 young, healthy adults performing one or a few laboratory protocols, and evaluate on held-out participants. With so few participants, analytical choices can easily change, or bias, the conclusions. Such choices include selecting training settings or the best of several training runs on test-participant results, fitting normalisation on data that include the test participant, evaluating one random initialisation as if it were representative, and handling missing sensor data ad hoc so that different input combinations are tested on different subsets of the data. In both of our preliminary studies, at least one of these practices was present. When the aim is to compare inputs rather than to maximise one model's score, these choices matter as much as the model. Comparisons should be made on identical data, with a training procedure fixed in advance and applied identically to every configuration, and with explicit estimates of the variability caused by random initialisation. Pooling cohorts increases the number and diversity of participants available for training and testing, but only if the data are processed identically.

In the present study, we therefore pooled the two datasets and re-analysed both from the raw recordings with one harmonised processing and evaluation pipeline. Together, the cohorts comprise 27 healthy adults and seven cycling protocols, i.e. ramp-incremental tests, PRBS exercise spanning the moderate and heavy intensity domains, interval and step exercise, with breath-by-breath V̇O₂ as ground truth. Inputs were grouped into five domains that correspond to distinct sensors or physiological systems: external work rate, HR (from a chest strap), ventilation (V̇E and BR from the same strap, uncalibrated), peripheral muscle oxygenation (NIRS) and anthropometrics (sex and skeletal muscle mass). Data quality was assessed with explicit, predefined rules per sensor and per protocol segment, and every input configuration was trained and tested on exactly the same seconds of data. A single TCN architecture and training procedure was fixed before the main analyses and applied to every model, every model was trained with five random seeds without selection, and performance was estimated with leave-one-subject-out cross-validation.

The primary aim of this study was to determine which input modalities, alone and in combination, give the most accurate continuous prediction of V̇O₂ during cycling, when evaluated on unseen participants. To this end, we compared all 46 combinations of the five input domains. Each domain corresponds to a sensor that a practitioner would have to buy, wear and maintain.
